## Where this fits

So far the training loop has been drawn as a neat sequence: generate, score, update, repeat. This section is about what happens when you have to make that **fast** on real hardware. Lambert: "this is the stuff that I really know more than loss functions."

The core tension:

> **The math assumes strictly on-policy training (generate → update → generate → update). Real systems break that a little, on purpose, because strictly on-policy wastes most of the GPUs' time.**

---

## Slide: "On-policy vs. off-policy"

**In theory (on-policy):** generate → update → generate → update. Each batch is generated by the current model, used for one or a few epochs, then thrown away. Lambert: "the math only works if you're doing this kind of on-policy sampling idea." The policy gradient from lecture 3 assumes the samples come from the model being updated.

**In reality (async):** generation and training **overlap on different groups of GPUs**. The model doing the generating may be **1 to N steps behind** the model being trained.

**The trade-off:** perfect on-policy is slow, because GPUs sit idle while waiting for each other. **Slight staleness is usually fine.**

### Why generation is the bottleneck *(my addition, for context)*

Generating text is slow: the model writes **one token at a time**, and long reasoning answers can be thousands of tokens. Training on a finished batch processes all tokens in parallel and is comparatively fast. So in a strictly alternating system, the training GPUs spend much of their time waiting for generation to finish.

### Why staleness is survivable

This is where lecture 3's machinery pays off. If the generating model is a few steps behind, the batch is slightly off-policy, which is the same situation as PPO's later epochs and minibatches. **The ratio ρ (current ÷ the model that generated the batch) and clipping are built to handle exactly that mismatch.** Lecture 3 `02` noted that "old log-probs" are often just the **generation-time log-probs**. In an async system that's literally true: π_old is whatever slightly-stale model did the generating.

## Slide: "Synchronous vs. asynchronous training"

*(The diagram isn't in the folder. This is reconstructed from the caption and the transcript.)*

There are three ways to arrange the GPUs:

```
1. SYNCHRONOUS, same GPUs (alternate):
   GPUs: [generate][train][generate][train][generate][train]
         one job at a time, simple, slow

2. SYNCHRONOUS, separate GPU groups (still on-policy):
   gen GPUs:   [generate]        [generate]        [generate]
   train GPUs:           [train]           [train]           [train]
         each group idles while waiting for the other

3. ASYNCHRONOUS (off-policy by a step or so):
   gen GPUs:   [generate][generate][generate][generate][generate]
   train GPUs:    [train][train][train][train][train][train]
         both always busy; generation uses slightly older weights
```

The caption: "Synchronous training idles GPUs; separating generation and training helps, but on-policy requires waiting. Async (off-policy) overlaps both for full utilization."

Lambert: "the big thing to get modern RL to work at scale is you have two different batches of GPUs doing two different operations, and you want them all to be running all the time very efficiently."

## Slide: "Asynchronous RL training"

Modern RL for LLMs splits the compute into two roles:

| Role | Hardware | Job | Software |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Actors** | Inference GPUs | Generate completions | **vLLM** or similar, a fast text-generation engine |
| **Learners** | Training GPUs | Compute the loss, gradient and optimizer step | The training code from `01`–`03` |

A process-management library (e.g. **Ray**) coordinates them.

*(The diagram isn't in the folder. This is from the caption and the transcript.)*

```
                  ┌──────────── prompt queue ────────────┐
                  │                                      ▼
   LEARNERS (training GPUs)                     ACTORS (inference GPUs, many)
   compute updates                              generate completions
                  ▲                                      │
                  └──────────── results queue ◄──────────┘

                  ═══ periodic weight sync: learner ──► actors ═══
```

1. The learner puts prompts on a **prompt queue**.
2. Actors take prompts, generate completions, and put them on a **results queue**.
3. The learner pulls finished completions from the results queue and trains on them.
4. **Every so often, the learner's updated weights are copied to the actors** (not pictured on the slide). Between syncs, actors generate with slightly old weights, and that's the staleness.

Lambert: actors "end up being the compute bottleneck," so you typically have many of them. There are "a lot of tricks to make sure that this general system is fast," and you "could do whole lectures on RL infra."

### Connecting to the timeline

In lecture 3's timeline, steps 1–5 (generate, score, compute advantages) and steps 6–7 (loss, update) were one sequence. **Async infrastructure runs them as a pipeline**: while the learner does steps 6–7 on batch *n*, the actors are already doing steps 1–2 for batch *n+1* (or *n+2*) with the weights from the last sync.

For multi-turn tool use (lecture 11), the actors also drive pools of **sandboxes**. Rollout lengths get a long tail, weight syncs can land in the middle of one rollout, and GRPO waits for the slowest of its G. See the actors-plus-sandboxes diagram in `../lecture-11-tools-and-agents/04-training-for-tool-use-and-takeaways.md`.

## The whole section in four lines

1. **The math wants strict on-policy** (generate, update, repeat), but that leaves GPUs idle, since generation is slow and token-by-token.
2. **Real systems split GPUs into actors** (generate with vLLM) **and learners** (train), connected by prompt/result queues, coordinated by something like Ray, with **periodic weight syncs** from learner to actors.
3. **This makes training slightly off-policy:** generation uses weights 1–N steps old. That's usually fine, because the ratio and clipping were built for this mismatch.
4. **Codebases:** start with **TRL**. Open Instruct for research recipes. veRL and OpenRLHF are popular for RLVR.
